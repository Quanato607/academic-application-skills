# Research Taste And Trajectory

Use this reference when the letter must show why the candidate has PhD-level research potential.

## Research Taste Signals

A strong letter should show the candidate can do at least two of these:

- Reframe an implementation task into a research question.
- Notice when aggregate metrics hide important failure modes.
- Design an evaluation that better matches the real use case.
- Treat negative results as evidence, not just failure.
- Identify what is missing in the current formulation.
- Balance methodological novelty with domain constraints.
- Move from preliminary work to stronger formulation and validation.

## Trajectory Pattern

Do not write a CV list. Convert outputs into a growth claim.

Weak:

> The candidate has papers at A, B, and C.

Strong:

> Across these projects, the candidate repeatedly returned to a difficult research problem, refined the formulation, strengthened the evaluation, and pushed the work toward more competitive venues.

## Direct vs Indirect Scope

When the recommender directly supervised only one project:

- Write that project in detail.
- Mention other publications as broader trajectory only.
- Do not imply the recommender observed all other projects.

Safe wording:

> The candidate's broader research trajectory reinforces this impression.

Avoid:

> In all of these projects, I observed him...

## Field-Agnostic Translation

For any field, map the candidate's work into:

- Core problem: what recurring problem are they trying to solve?
- Taste: what did they notice that a routine implementer would miss?
- Ownership: how did they change the direction of the work?
- Evidence: what outputs prove the work matured?
- Future fit: what kind of open-ended PhD problem can they handle?

## Example Research-Taste Moves

- Health sciences: aggregate performance hides subgroup, missing-data, or deployment-setting failure.
- AI and NLP: headline accuracy hides reasoning, retrieval, calibration, or interaction failures.
- Robotics: success rate hides unsafe recovery behavior.
- Biology: aggregate phenotype hides subgroup-specific mechanisms.
- Social science: average treatment effect hides heterogeneous causal pathways.
- Humanities: broad thematic reading hides archive-specific interpretive constraints.
